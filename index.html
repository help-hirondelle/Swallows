Swallows in Our Cities

House martins and barn swallows are priority conservation species in Switzerland and Vaud. Their decline is not caused by one single problem, but by a chain of urban changes: fewer nesting sites, less mud, fewer insects, renovation pressure, and lower tolerance when droppings appear under nests.

Buildings are habitat

House martins nest under roof overhangs on facades. Barn swallows use barns, stables, hangars, garages, corridors, and other semi-open spaces.

The city changed

Smooth facades, sealed openings, paved ground, and the loss of traditional architectural features make nesting harder.

Coexistence is the project

Protecting swallows also means reducing conflict around hygiene, maintenance, and aesthetics so residents can tolerate colonies.

Understand

Why Swallows Need Protection

Swallows are emblematic birds of towns, villages, and rural buildings. They are often associated with spring, but their presence is becoming less secure because the built environment no longer provides enough safe places to nest and feed.

They return each spring

Swallows often return to former nesting sites. Protecting existing colonies is therefore one of the most reliable conservation actions.

They regulate insects

Swallows feed almost entirely on flying insects. One barn swallow pair may need around 150,000 flies, about 1 kg of food, to raise a brood.

They reveal urban health

Their decline is also a warning sign of broader biodiversity loss in urban and rural ecosystems.

Main threats

Loss of nesting sites

Renovations, demolitions, sealed openings, smoother facades, and disappearing roof overhangs reduce places where nests can hold.

Lack of mud

Paved roads and impermeable surfaces remove the moist soil swallows need to build and repair nests.

Decline of insects

Intensive agriculture, pesticides, light pollution, and the loss of green areas reduce flying insect food.

Breeding-season disturbance

Construction, facade work, or blocked access during nesting can interrupt breeding and feeding.

Droppings conflict

Droppings under nests are one of the main reasons people complain, lose tolerance, or remove and block nests.

Fragmented action

Current measures often arrive too late, are poorly coordinated, or are not maintained after installation.

Nesting

Nesting Behaviour and the Importance of Mud

Swallows depend heavily on human-made structures. If a traditional nesting site disappears through renovation, demolition, or facade modification, the birds may struggle to relocate successfully.

House martins

House martins build mud nests under roof overhangs or on rough facades. A surface that is too smooth prevents natural nests from attaching properly.

Barn swallows

Barn swallows usually nest inside or partly inside buildings: barns, stables, hangars, garages, laundry rooms, corridors, and covered passages can all be used.

Openings matter

For new barn swallow sites, a large opening helps colonisation. Once a nest is occupied, a smaller entrance of about 10 cm can be enough.

Architecture matters

Overhangs of around 30 cm, sheltered placement, facade roughness, and open urban space can improve nesting; nearby vegetation can increase predator risk in some setups.

Mud is a breeding resource

A natural nest can require roughly 750 to 1,400 mud pellets. A useful mud area should be within about 200 m of the nesting site, open and flat, protected from disturbance, and made of clay-rich soil rather than sandy material.

Mud areas need maintenance. During the main nest-building period, roughly from mid-April to mid-June, the soil must stay moist and vegetation should be removed manually or mechanically without pesticides.

Act

How to Help Swallows

The most effective action is to protect existing colonies. New solutions can help, but they work best when planned early, placed well, maintained, and coordinated across buildings or communes.

Residents

If there is an active nest

Do not remove or block it. Manage nuisance with communication, cleaning routines, and droppings boards where appropriate.

Construction

Before works begin

Inspect buildings for nests, schedule work outside breeding periods, restore access, and plan replacement nests early when unavoidable.

Found bird

If a swallow is on the ground

Injured adults need wildlife care. If an uninjured young bird has fallen, returning it directly to the nest is often best.

Droppings boards

Reduce soiling without removing nests

Boards placed about 60 cm below nests can reduce nuisance while keeping nests in place. They must be wide enough, accessible, and cleaned regularly; neglected boards can create hygiene problems, help predators reach nests, or attract pigeons.

Artificial nests

Add nesting support where conditions fit

Artificial nests can help near existing colonies or priority areas where natural nesting surfaces were lost. They are not universal: placement, overhangs, disturbance, and maintenance affect success.

Planning

Renovation, Construction, and Communal Action

Swallow protection is easiest when it is considered before work starts. Municipalities are especially important because they can scale action, coordinate across communes, and reduce complaints, cleaning costs, and conflict.

Before renovation

Check buildings for nests before work begins. If active nests are present, works should normally be planned outside the breeding season.

Timing

Barn swallows usually arrive in March and leave around October. House martins usually arrive in April and leave by the end of September. Renovation is ideally planned between late September and early April.

Replacement planning

If nests must be removed for unavoidable works, replacement nesting sites should be planned nearby before the birds return. Swallow towers or martin hotels may be considered for large colonies, but they are expensive, often perform poorly, and should not be used as an excuse to remove successful building colonies.

Local action plans

Communes can map existing observations, update old data, define areas to maintain, reinforce, or create, and track complaint rates, cleaning effort, installed solutions, and occupancy.

Legal and care

Before You Touch a Nest

Swallows and their nests are protected

In Switzerland, protected birds must not be killed, injured, captured, or have their eggs, nests, or breeding sites destroyed or removed. Nests should be checked before renovation, demolition, facade work, or maintenance.

Plan works outside the breeding season and verify local procedure before acting.

Protection is about reproduction

A project that removes nests, blocks access, or transforms a building so swallows can no longer use it can have serious consequences for a colony.

If you find a swallow

Adult birds on the ground are often weakened or injured and should be brought to a wildlife care centre. A young bird that is not injured is often best placed back in its nest.

Small building choices can protect the spring

Swallows need mud, insects, and safe places to return to. A city that welcomes swallows is a city that welcomes biodiversity.
